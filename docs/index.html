Australian National Parks Tourism

By Nicholas Ong · October 2026

Introduction

This story investigates visitor numbers to Australia’s national parks and protected areas. It compares visitation across parks and across seasons, looks for long-term tourism trends, and explores how outdoor tourism has changed over time.

Why does tourism to National Parks in Australia matter?
It matters because it plays an important role to Australia's economy, environment and local communities. Most people think that Australia's national parks are busiest during summer and school holidays. Let us go through the numbers in visits, parks and routes, to see whether this is true.

The story moves through five parts:

  1. 01 What does the landscape in Australia look like?
  2. 02 What changed in Queensland's parks?
  3. 03 How does the tourism flow?
  4. 04 Over time, how do visitors reach park regions?
  5. 05 How's business in park regions?

Section 1

What does the landscape in Australia look like?

Australia's protected areas differs in size and location.
These maps show where national parks are found,
how much land is protected in each state and territory,
and how governance patterns differ across the country.

Map 1

More parks east, bigger parks inland

Start with the large circle in inland South Australia.
Circle size show park area, bigger means more hectares.
Many small circles cluster on the east coast;
whereas, the largest parks is located in the interior and north.

Darker circles mark older parks; brighter ones are newer.
Both appear on the coast and interior.
Large, lighter inland circles suggest that some large
areas became national parks recently.

The map shows where parks are and how much land they cover. But who are the ones looking after them? The next map focuses on organisations responsible for the protected areas in Australia.

Map 2

Highest counts: government and private

Compare Government and Private first: darker cells
mean more protected areas on the same count scale.
Both have high counts along the east coast and in
the southwest; Government is also strong in the southeast.

Who sets entry rules, maintains trails and provides
facilities under each type of governance? Could this
affect public access, permit requirements and
services available when tourists arrive at a protected area?

These maps count protected areas, not their size.
The next map asks a different question: how much of
each state's total land area are national parks?

Map 3

National park land ranges from 3.4% to 33%

Look first at the ACT: 33% of its land is national parks, the highest share shown. Western Australia has the lowest share at 3.4%. Tasmania leads states at 21.8%, while the Northern Territory is also low at 4.3%.

A larger protected share does not mean more visitors. This map shows the proportion of land that is a national park, but not visitor numbers, access or tourism demand. It compares land coverage, instead of a destination's popularity.

Queensland combines a low protected share, 5.5%, with clusters of national parks along its east coast. Section 2 looks more closely at its parks and visitors.

Section 2

What changed in Queensland's parks?

Map 3 showed that just 5.5% of Queensland's land was protected.
Now we ask how that share has changed over time.
Has protected land expanded or reduced, and when?
This section shows the changes behind today's snapshot in Queensland.

Visualisation 4

Main national park category reduced by 9.8%

Queensland's main national park category lost 9.8%
of its area between 2008 and 2023/24, roughly 1/10th.
Cape York, Aboriginal land, scientific and recovery
parks are separate categories in this comparison.

Cape York national parks stand out, growing by
1,267.2% from 2009. Nature refuges also grew by 789.6%.

Each panel has its own hectare scale, compare line
lengths within a panel, not between the two panels.

Does this change which parks visitors can enter,
or what they can do there? Area only cannot tell us.

Visualisation 5

Cape York parks climbed to 5th place

Cape York parks grew from 8th in 2008 to 5th
in 2013. Recovery parks disappear as categories were
reclassified in 2014; scientific parks are back in 2016,
taking sixth place. The ranks here count parks, not area.

Protected land provides the setting for park tourism.
But how many people visit, and where do they go?
Section 3 goes from the parks to their visitors.

Section 3

How does the tourism flow?

Protected land is only one part of the story.
Next we see where overnight travellers go, how much
each trip is in regional spending, and which states
they come from. The focus is on regions containing parks.

Visualisation 6

North Coast NSW leads overnight trips

North Coast NSW leads in 2024 with about 6.2 million overnight trips, ahead of South Coast at 4.5 million. Gold Coast follows at 4.3 million and Sunshine Coast at 4 million, whereas each remaining region is below 3.3 million.

Read the percentage labels beside each pair of bars. North Coast NSW grew 10.8% and Queensland Country 8.9%. High Country dropped by 7.0%, South Coast 6.9% and Canberra 6.1%. Five regions rose and five fell.

Only the top 10 of 30 selected park regions are shown. These are overnight trips to regions, not park visits.

Visualisation 7

Gold Coast outspends 5 major cities

Gold Coast averages $1,344 per overnight trip, above five of the six cities; only Darwin is higher at $1,877. The other 9 park regions sit below all 6 cities, from Sunshine Coast at $981 to Queensland Country at $535.

Could trip length, accommodation or activities explain the spending gap? These numbers cover regional spending only, rather than the full cost of an overnight trip.

Visualisation 8

NSW leads interstate overnight trips

Start by hovering to New South Wales on the left. It sends 11.9 million interstate trips and receives 11.7 million, leading both ways. Victoria is 2nd for outgoing trips; Queensland is next for arrivals.

Grey ribbons show trips whose origin or destination was not published or could not be assigned in the data.

Interstate trips depend on transport links between states. Section 4 then shows how visitors reach their destinations.

Section 4

Over time, how do visitors reach park regions?

Air routes offer a way of access to park regions. We analyse passenger traffic since 2014, from the COVID collapse to recovery, then compare gateway airports month by month to see which airport is busiest in the year.

Visualisation 9

Gold Coast to Sydney remains the busiest

Before COVID, traffic fluctuated, with growth on some routes. Passenger numbers collapsed in 2020, following an uneven recovery. Gold Coast routes came back strongly, while Canberra-Melbourne remained below its earlier levels.

Recovery differs by route. Click Gold Coast-Melbourne in the legend to focus on its comeback, then compare Canberra-Melbourne's lower passenger levels.

Visualisation 10

Cairns peaks in July; Hobart in January

Read each row: July peaks at Cairns, Townsville, Karratha and Mackay; October at Hamilton Island, Proserpine, Rockhampton. Jan leads at Gold Coast and Sunshine Coast, Launceston and Hobart; Dec at Ballina, Nov at Canberra.

Could weather and school holidays help explain these peaks? Busier airports could mean more demand nearby, but passenger movements alone do not measure park crowds.

Section 5

How's business in park regions?

Visitors need places to stay, meals and other services. Tourism businesses help meet that demand. Compare the shares of companies in each size band across park regions and comparison regions to see how their economy varies.

Visualisation 11

Higher employer share in park regions

Compare the non-employing and small-business bars. Businesses without staff account for 44.2% in park regions, vs 51.7% in comparison regions. Small employers make up 20.6% versus 15.6%, a larger share in park regions.

A higher share of employers could support local jobs. Could the reliance on park visitors also leave these businesses more vulnerable to seasonal demand and travel disruptions?

Conclusion

North Coast NSW was 1st in regional overnight trips in 2024. Gateway peaks vary: Cairns in July, Hobart in January. Regional growth was mixed, with 5 rising and 5 falling. Air traffic recovered (albeit unevenly) after COVID.

Visualisations 4 and 5 show shifts in Queensland's protected land, with Cape York parks expanding and rising. Visualisation 9 shows air travel's COVID collapse and uneven comeback.

Trip comparisons cover only 2023-24 and ten regions. Air data contains gaps. Trips and flights do not measure park visits, crowds or spending within parks.
