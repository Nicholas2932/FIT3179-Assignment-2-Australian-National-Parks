Australian National Parks Tourism

By Nicholas Ong · October 2026

Introduction

This story investigates visitor numbers to Australia’s national parks and protected areas. It compares visitation across parks and across seasons, looks for long-term trends.

Why does tourism to national parks in Australia matter? It matters because it plays an important role in Australia's economy, environment and local communities. Most people think that Australia's national parks are busiest during summer and school holidays. Let look at the numbers on parks, visitors and routes, to see whether this is true.

The story moves through five parts:

  1. 01 What does the landscape in Australia look like?
  2. 02 What changed in Queensland's parks?
  3. 03 How does tourism flow?
  4. 04 How do visitors reach park regions, and when?
  5. 05 How's business in park regions?

Section 1

What does the landscape in Australia look like?

Australia's protected areas differ in size and location. These maps show where national parks are found, how much land is national park in each state and territory, and how governance patterns differ across the country.

Map 1

More parks east, bigger parks inland

Start with the large circle in inland South Australia. Circle size shows park area: bigger means more hectares. Many small circles cluster on the east coast whereas, the largest parks are in the interior and north.

Darker circles mark older parks; lighter ones are newer. Both appear on the coast and in the interior. Large, lighter inland circles suggest that some big areas became national parks recently.

The map shows where parks are and how much land they cover. But who looks after them? The next map focuses on the types of governance responsible for the protected areas in Australia.

Map 2

Highest counts: government and private

Compare Government and Private first: darker cells mean more protected areas on the same count scale. Both have high counts along the east coast and in the southwest; Government is also strong in the southeast.

Who sets entry rules, maintains trails and provides facilities under each type of governance? Could this affect public access, permit requirements and services available when tourists arrive at a protected area?

These maps count protected areas, not their size. The next map asks a different question: how much of each state and territory's total land is national park?

Map 3

National park share of land ranges from 3.4% to 45.2%

Look first at the ACT: 45.2% of its land is national parks, the highest share shown. Western Australia has the lowest share at 3.4%. Tasmania leads states at 21.8%, while the Northern Territory is also low at 4.3%.

A larger protected share does not mean more visitors. This map shows the proportion of land that is a national park, but not visitor numbers, access or tourism demand. It compares land coverage, not popularity.

Queensland combines a low protected share, 5.5%, with clusters of national parks along its east coast. Section 2 looks more closely at its parks and visitors.

Section 2

What changed in Queensland's parks?

Map 3 showed that just 5.5% of Queensland's land is national park. Now we ask how that share has changed over time. Has protected land expanded or shrunk, and when? This section shows the changes behind today's snapshot in Queensland.

Visualisation 4

Main national park category shrank by 9.8%

Queensland's main national park category lost 9.8% of its area between 2008 and 2023/24, roughly 1/10th. Cape York, Aboriginal land, scientific and recovery parks are separate categories in this comparison.

Cape York national parks stand out, growing by 789.6% since 2009. Nature refuges also grew by 1,267.2%.

Does this change which parks visitors can enter, or what they can do there? Area alone cannot tell us.

Visualisation 5

Cape York parks climbed to 5th place

Cape York parks grew from 8th in 2008 to 5th in 2013. Recovery parks disappeared when categories were reclassified in 2014; scientific parks returned in 2016, in 6th place. The ranks here count parks, not area.

Protected land provides the setting for park tourism. But how many people visit, and where do they go? Section 3 goes from the parks to their visitors.

Section 3

How does tourism flow?

Protected land is only one part of the story. Next we see where overnight travellers go, how much each trip is worth in regional spending, and which states they come from. The focus is on regions containing parks.

Visualisation 6

The North Coast NSW leads overnight trips

The North Coast NSW leads in 2024 with about 6.2 million overnight trips, ahead of the South Coast at 4.5 million. The Gold Coast follows at 4.3 million and the Sunshine Coast at 4.0 million; every other region is below 3.3 million.

Read the percentage labels beside each pair of bars. The North Coast NSW grew 10.8% and Queensland Country 8.9%. High Country dropped by 7.0%, the South Coast 6.9% and Canberra 6.1%. Five regions rose and five fell.

Only the top 10 of 30 selected park regions are shown. These are overnight trips to regions, not park visits.

Visualisation 7

The Gold Coast outspends 5 capital cities

The Gold Coast averages $1,344 per overnight trip, above 5 of the 6 cities; only Darwin is higher at $1,877. The other 9 park regions sit below all 6 cities, from The Sunshine Coast at $981 to Queensland Country at $535.

Could trip length, accommodation or activities explain the spending gap? These numbers cover regional spending only, rather than the full cost of an overnight trip.

Visualisation 8

NSW leads interstate overnight trips

Start by hovering over New South Wales on the left. It sends 11.9 million interstate trips and receives 11.7 million, leading both ways. Victoria is second for outgoing trips; Queensland is next for arrivals.

Grey ribbons show trips whose origin or destination was not published or could not be assigned in the data.

Interstate trips depend on transport links between states. Section 4 shows how visitors reach their destinations.

Section 4

How do visitors reach park regions, and when?

Air routes are one way to reach park regions. We analyse passenger traffic since 2014, from the COVID collapse to recovery, then compare gateway airports month by month to see when in the year each airport is busiest.

Visualisation 9

The Gold Coast to Sydney remains the busiest

Before COVID, traffic fluctuated, with growth on some routes. Passenger numbers collapsed in 2020, followed by an uneven recovery. The Gold Coast routes came back strongly, while Canberra-Melbourne remained below its earlier levels.

Recovery differs by route. Click on the Gold Coast-Melbourne route in the legend to focus on its comeback, then compare Canberra-Melbourne's lower passenger levels.

Visualisation 10

Cairns peaks in July; The Sunshine Coast in January

Read each row: July peaks at Cairns, Townsville, Karratha and Mackay; October at Hamilton Island, Proserpine, and Rockhampton. Jan leads at the Gold Coast and the Sunshine Coast, Launceston and Hobart; Dec at Ballina, Nov at Canberra.

Could weather and school holidays help explain these peaks? Busier airports could mean more demand nearby, but passenger movements alone do not measure park crowds.

Section 5

How's business in park regions?

Visitors need places to stay, meals and other services. Tourism businesses help meet that demand. Compare the shares of businesses to see how their economy differ.

Visualisation 11

Higher employer share in park regions

Compare the non-employing and small-business bars. Businesses without staff account for 44.2% in park regions, vs 51.7% in comparison regions. Small employers make up 20.6% vs 15.6%, a larger share in park regions.

A higher share of employers could support local jobs. Could relying on park visitors also leave these businesses more vulnerable to seasonal demand and travel disruptions?

Conclusion

The North Coast NSW was first in regional overnight trips in 2024. Gateway peaks vary: Cairns in July, Hobart in January. Regional growth was mixed, with five of the ten regions rising and five falling.

Visualisations 4 and 5 show shifts in Queensland's protected land, with Cape York parks expanding and rising. Visualisation 9 shows air travel's COVID collapse and uneven comeback.

Trip comparisons cover only 2023-24 and ten regions. Air data contains gaps. Trips and flights do not measure park visits, crowds or spending within parks.

Overall, many Queensland gateways peak in winter, especially during July. However, gateways peak most during Janurary, which means it is true that national park gateways have more visitors during the summer.
